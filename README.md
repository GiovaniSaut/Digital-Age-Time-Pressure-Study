# Time Pressure in the Digital Age — Undergraduate Research Project

**BSc Psychology Dissertation | LJMU | 2025–2026**

---

## Overview

Does using more technology make us feel less rushed or more?

This study examined whether the *quality* of someone's digital experience predicts **time pressure** beyond what personality alone explains. 100 participants completed a survey measuring two dimensions of time pressure, procrastination, locus of control, and four dimensions of digital experience (immersion, efficiency, social connectedness, wellbeing).

---

## Key Findings

**1. The Efficiency Paradox 🔄**
People who reported that technology helps them work faster also reported *higher* awareness of time shortage — not lower. Time saved by productivity tools doesn't become free time; it gets immediately filled by new demands. Apps that track and quantify time may also make scarcity more visible, reinforcing the pressure rather than relieving it.

**2. Personality beats technology 🧠**
Procrastination was the strongest predictor of time pressure, explaining up to 60% of variance in feeling emotionally rushed. Digital variables added only modest explanatory power. Giving people better productivity tools without addressing self-regulation is unlikely to reduce stress.

**3. Cognitive and emotional time pressure have different causes 📊**
*Knowing* you're short on time and *feeling* emotionally rushed are driven by different mechanisms — and require different interventions. Digital efficiency predicted the cognitive dimension only; personality predicted both.

**4. Gender reversal among digital natives 👥**
Men reported significantly higher cognitive awareness of time shortage than women — the opposite of established research. The sample was predominantly young students (avg age 20) with no caregiving responsibilities, suggesting this may reflect cohort-specific patterns among digital natives.

## Why it matters

This has direct relevance for **workplace wellbeing** (productivity tool rollouts may increase cognitive load without reducing stress), **product teams** building time-management apps (efficiency gains may heighten rather than reduce felt pressure), and **HR analytics** (procrastination and control beliefs predict distress more strongly than technology use).
